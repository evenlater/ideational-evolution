William Ewart Gladstone's diary entry for November 24, 1858 describes his arrival by ship in the Ionian Islands, sailing toward Corfu for his strange new job as Lord High Commissioner Extraordinary of the tiny British colonies there -- an assignment that led to ridicule from enemies and embarrassment from friends. He noted the coastline and mused, in his diary, that Homer had probably had the same misty view in mind when writing of it. The moment is telling: Gladstone was thinking of Odysseus's own approach to that stretch of coast in Book Five of *The Odyssey*, whose landfall he quoted in his diary via Pope's translation.

That Gladstone had Homer on his mind while he sailed into the mist is not a big surprise. Corfu fascinated Gladstone, a sometime Homeric scholar, because it was believed to be the land where Homer's Odysseus told his tall tales of battling a Cyclops, and a witch, sea monsters, and giants to an audience of Phaecians. Homer was no casual obsession for Gladstone. Just eight months before he had published a massive, three-volume, 1,600-page treatise entitled *Studies on Homer and the Homeric Age* (Magnus 122). Homeric scholars scoffed -- one Oxford don dismissed it outright (Aldous 97-98). But Gladstone was besotted with Odysseus, and it was in his writings on Homer that he most clearly articulated an ideological transformation from conservative to liberal on some of the top issues of the day -- an ideological transformation that was actualized just three months after his trip to Corfu ended, when he joined the ragtag coalition of radicals, liberals, and Whigs that would become the Liberal Party and eventually be led by Gladstone for decades. In Corfu something changed in him that led Gladstone, previously labeled "the last hope of the stern unbending Tories" by Thomas Babington Macaulay, to become known widely as "The People's William" -- one of his era's primary definitions of liberalism. Gladstone scholar David Bebbington has described *Studies of Homer and the Homeric Age* as effectively the high-water mark of Gladstone's intellectual life as a Conservative (Bebbington 177).

Historians have debated for more than a century why Gladstone made the decisions to go to Corfu and to join the liberals. But few have examined the impact of Corfu on his ideological transformation. This comic episode, coming as it did after four long years out of high office, was a major factor that resulted in his sudden decision just weeks later to leave the Tories. He arrived in Corfu a politically isolated conservative. He left it as a seedling of a new liberal.

Why did Gladstone, a singular young star on Britain's parliamentary stage, accept this comically quixotic assignment 1,400 miles from the political scene in London? Both contemporaries and historians have speculated the whole thing was a set-up by his rival, Benjamin Disraeli, and Disraeli's best friend Edward Bulwer-Lytton. Disraeli is reported to have said as much at the time -- something to the effect of, now that they'd gotten Gladstone down, they intended to keep him there -- and the remark, once it reached Gladstone, reportedly fed his growing hostility toward the Conservative Party.

Gladstone's friends thought the decision to go to Corfu was farcical. Richard Aldous, in his 2006 double biography *The Lion and the Unicorn: Gladstone vs. Disraeli*, reports that they viewed the incident as resembling something out of Gilbert and Sullivan (Aldous 110). Gladstone should have stayed home, they said, and built on his position as a star in the parliamentary scene. Former Prime Minister Lord John Russell called the mission to Corfu "a curious one" and even suggested Gladstone had accepted the assignment in order to be absent on discussions of electoral reform. Gladstone friend and fellow Peelite Sir James Graham called the mission "a sad mess," and warned that if Gladstone tried to sever his connection to Disraeli, "Dizzy's harpoon will be in him" (Hawkins 84).

## Historiography

What was going on in Gladstone's head? He had decided to accept his "Lilliputian" destiny, he told a friend. But why? For their part, historians have debated why Gladstone took the assignment for almost two centuries. Travis L. Crosby's *The Two Mr. Gladstones* lays out the historiography of that question more comprehensively than anyone else, and it's worth pausing on him as a guide here.

Crosby surveys the field: Morley points to the decline of Catherine Gladstone's health after the death of her sister. Matthew argues Gladstone understood the islands' strategic importance amid European rivalries in the eastern Mediterranean. Pointon suggests intellectual curiosity -- a chance to continue his Homeric research somewhere congenial. Shannon instead sees a frustrated, restless Gladstone for whom the Ionian post was simply an opportunity to escape. Crosby's own view is that all of these may have played a part, but that above all the mission represented a kind of ladder out of Gladstone's political isolation -- a resolution to it, or at minimum a restful limbo, and in any case a way to remove himself entirely from his sources of stress in England (Crosby 91).

But it was Aldous who offered the sharpest version of the setup theory, concluding it's hard to escape the sense that the whole Ionian adventure was an elaborate trap (Aldous 110). This was a popular view among the moderate Peelite wing of the Conservative Party, which supported Gladstone because he was one of them. Taking the post meant vacating his Commons seat, and HCG Matthew notes in a footnote to his *Gladstone 1809-1898* that some Peelites saw the whole affair as a Disraelian plot to force Gladstone into a by-election he'd likely lose (Matthew 165) -- meaning the underlying motive, on this reading, was simply to get rid of him.

The recollections of an undersecretary in Bulwer-Lytton's Colonial Office clinch the interpretation. As Crosby reports it, when the Colonial Secretary asked his advisor Lord Carnarvon for a recommendation, Carnarvon named Gladstone for his abilities and his lack of party ties at that moment; Disraeli agreed that the posting's classical aesthetic suited Gladstone well, but added, more revealingly, that with Gladstone down, they meant to keep him down. Crosby notes in a footnote that these words were repeated back to Gladstone, infuriating him, and that Carnarvon later believed this was the real turning point in Gladstone's break with the Conservatives (Crosby 90, 241).

Thus, one catty remark from Disraeli confirms two things at once via a first-hand witness: first, that Gladstone's trip to Corfu was the result of Disraeli's mischief, and second, that it resulted directly in Gladstone's accepting a new home on the Left.

## Ionian Politics

After seeing the "mirage" of mist on the horizon of Corfu, Gladstone launched into a busy day -- landing to military honors, four hours of briefing on Ionian politics, a dinner party that evening, and, as his diary records almost comically, beginning to translate his own address into Italian.

We know what Gladstone was there to do, if not why he wanted the gig. Colonial Secretary Bulwer-Lytton had offered him the post of Lord High Commissioner Extraordinary, tasked with advising the crown on what to do with these small colonial possessions while the local population agitated to rejoin Greece. Bulwer-Lytton framed the assignment to Gladstone as a chance to reconcile a Greek-speaking people to the practice of liberty -- a noble episode for his career (Morley 594).

Gladstone accepted with visible ambivalence, describing the decision to a correspondent as one he'd been more or less swept into by circumstance, leaving him no honorable way to decline, and confessing that even a small amount of deliberation had cost him an enormous toll of anxiety (Shannon 365).

The assignment may have been Lilliputian, but the consequences were in some ways Brobdingnagian.

Corfu was, and still is, a 35-by-11-mile island with a population of 100,000. It, like the rest of the Ionian Islands, is now part of Greece, having been ceded to that nation by Britain in 1864. The islands had become part of the British empire in the aftermath of the Napoleonic Wars in 1815. However, Greece won its independence in 1830, after which the people of the Ionian Islands began campaigning for what they called *Enosis*, or union with Greece. The situation was similar to that of Italy, which at the time was a group of nation-states with different monarchical rulers, including some under the thumb of the Austrian empire. (This comparison was surely not lost on the people of the Ionian Islands when, for example, Verdi's opera *Nabucco*, a symbol of Italian unification, was performed on Corfu in 1844.) Gladstone arrived with limited sympathy for Italian independence, and an ambivalence about the Ionian Islands' desire to join Greece. Journalist Richard Pine, writing in *The Irish Times* in 2014, argued that Gladstone's own report reflected exactly this bind -- torn between his duty to defend Britain's continued role and his personal sympathy, as a devoted philhellene, for the case for union with Greece -- and that his report ended up ambiguous enough to satisfy no one. Pine speculated the experience may have nudged Gladstone, two decades later, toward the concept of Irish Home Rule. It's my contention that the experience also moved him toward supporting self-government for the Italian peoples, and therefore to the Liberal Party.

Initially, the Ionians greeted Gladstone as their means to rally for union with Greece. John Morley, Gladstone's first major biographer and still today one of the key ones, describes his visit to the island of Cephalonia in vivid terms: a crowd of a thousand greeting him through drenching rain with cries for union with Greece and against the fifty-year "tyranny" of the protectorate, disaffected petitions thrust into his carriage, and a frail elderly archbishop -- described by Gladstone's secretary Arthur Gordon as seated in gold-and-purple robes, a long white beard flowing over them, flanked by a small court of black-robed priests -- tearfully repeating, again and again, the same lament for "this unhappy island" (Morley 604).

Gladstone came to see the existing arrangement as bad for both Britain and the islands, Morley writes, but circumstances made it impossible for him either to simply hand the islands off or to recommend crushing the local constitution by force; the only course left was to try to turn a merely nominal system of representative government into something real (Morley 611).

Morley is not the only biographer who celebrates Gladstone's achievement in Corfu. Bulwer-Lytton's biographer Leslie Mitchell writes that Gladstone devised a scheme giving the islanders substantial self-government through their existing elected assembly, and quotes Bulwer-Lytton declaring the whole affair a vindication of England's honor before the world -- proof that what Britain preached to despots, it also practiced itself (Mitchell 216).

His words are ironic: Bulwer-Lytton and Disraeli were both fans of Greek culture, but the former had doomed the mission's ability to respond to the public demand for union with Greece by requiring at the outset that no such union was to be accomplished. He also insisted that British public opinion would not allow anything that could be seen as Britain "giving up," and declared that the electorate was to be made up only of property-holders, with no full democracy to be enacted. Mitchell sees these constraints as reasonable boundaries that allowed for an authentically liberal arrangement, and credits the mission with quieting talk of union with Greece for good (Mitchell 215). But within four years, the islands did rejoin Greece, so the demand hardly faded away.

Indeed, other historians paint the trip as a failure, emphasizing what happened afterward: all of Gladstone's proposals were rejected by the Ionian assembly on February 16, 1859, and he left three days later privately admitting, in biographer Philip Magnus's account, that he had entirely failed to reconcile the islanders to continued British rule (Magnus 138). Matthew's footnotes to *The Gladstone Diaries* add a small, telling detail: the assembly declined even to see him off.

There was a reason Gladstone was not long for the islands. He had discovered that keeping the post in Corfu meant he would have to surrender his seat in Parliament, representing Oxford. Having just been granted the commission by Queen Victoria, he then had to turn around and ask her to rescind it. He was not going to give up that seat. He stood for re-election unopposed and kept his job.

Gladstone returned to London on March 8, but not before a stop in Italy, which he would later say was one of the top issues that provoked his switch to the Liberal Party (Morley 628). His anger at Disraeli apparently combined with a newfound sensitivity to the plight of oppressed nations to drive him to join the Liberals. Corfu started him on that path.

## Italy changes everything

Wrestling with the people demanding self-government and a union with Greece seems to have changed Gladstone. When he arrived in Corfu, he was negative about Italy's goal of unity. After he left, he headed straight to Italy and announced he would support it. Corfu had changed his mind. While in Italy he met with the prime minister of the kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, the Count of Cavour, who had modernized the northern Italian states' economies and would go on to become the leading architect of Italian unification. Historians agree that the meeting led to his support for Italy. Biographer Roy Jenkins writes that where Gladstone had previously been skeptical of Italian unification, by the spring of 1859 the excitement sweeping the north, combined with Cavour's persuasiveness, both settled his doubts and lit his enthusiasm.

Jenkins discounts the possibility that Corfu changed Gladstone's mind, but his argument proves the opposite: he insists that Gladstone's three hours with Cavour mattered more to his future, and British politics, than his three months in Corfu (Jenkins 198). But did Gladstone really make so major a transition in just three hours? This seems impossible. It's much more likely that Gladstone's perspective on Italy changed because the thousands of people lobbying him to unite the Ionian Islands with Greece changed his mind. Gladstone's enthusiastic embrace of all things Greek reportedly drove his British colleagues to distrust and ridicule him while he was in Corfu (Magnus 136). It's that enthusiasm that transferred to his view of Italy.

Regardless of what caused the switch, Gladstone himself said his enthusiasm for Italy led to his joining the new Liberal Party. He suddenly found common ground with Lord Palmerston, who was to be the prime minister in the new Liberal coalition after June 6, 1859. Lord Stanmore, in his biography of Sidney Herbert, describes how the state of Italian affairs reshaped party politics at home: through 1858, Gladstone had been drifting toward Lord Derby's government, had no appetite for further parliamentary reform or the secret ballot, but held a deep antipathy to Austria alongside deep sympathy for Italian unity -- and since the Conservative ministry was hostile to the Italian cause while Liberal leaders favored it, that pull alone reshaped Gladstone's whole political attitude (Stanmore 180).

Previously Gladstone had been no fan of Palmerston. Jenkins notes that the two men had genuinely agreed on almost nothing except Italy and a shared, if differently expressed, wariness about expanding the franchise -- and it was the prominence of those two issues, Italy above all, in the months right after Gladstone's return from Corfu that made Palmerston temporarily less objectionable to him than Disraeli, setting him on a path with lasting consequences (Jenkins 198).

One of those effects was that Gladstone's sympathy for oppressed people abroad led to his changing his mind about expanding the vote at home. W.E. Williams, in the only book devoted to Gladstone's transition to liberalism, argues that his sympathy for oppressed peoples abroad clearly came first and reshaped his domestic views, quoting Lord Stanmore's observation that reform paired with sympathy for Sardinia -- or Greece, or Italy -- suddenly seemed far more tolerable to Gladstone than it had shortly before, and that he began observing conditions at home with newfound sympathy.

Writing to Sir John Acton in 1864, Gladstone himself said that it was the sheer weight of the Italian question, and Britain's foreign policy toward it, that led him to join the Liberals without a moment's hesitation (Morley 628).

This transition to supporting domestic rights could not have happened in a mere three-hour meeting with Cavour. More likely, Corfu opened the door of Gladstone's sympathy for oppressed peoples, and Cavour had only to walk through it.

## Isolation breeds change

Gladstone's decisions to go to Corfu, and to switch to the Liberal Party, were also motivated by a desperation for a home in a party. He later described the thirteen years spanning the middle of his life as a period of being cast out of party connection altogether -- severed from his old party, unwilling to commit to a new one (Hawkins 261).

He had been out of high office for four years, since 1855 when he last resigned the post of Chancellor of the Exchequer. But his isolation had really gone on longer than that. Gladstone had for all intents and purposes left the Conservative Party in 1846, after his mentor Sir Robert Peel had ended the so-called Corn Laws, which imposed tariffs on imported grain and therefore -- according first to the liberal Whigs and then to Peel -- raised the cost of bread and other food. Gladstone supported Peel, and when Disraeli successfully led a rebellion against Peel in support of the Corn Laws, Gladstone left the party. He was a Peelite, not aligned with any party, when the trip to Corfu happened, and it was that desperation in part that had led him to accept the gig. But when he was offered a position in the government by Disraeli in 1858, he rejected it vociferously.

Gladstone's acceptance of the new role with the Liberals was also probably motivated by the age of his new superiors. Palmerston and Russell were both near the end of their careers, while Disraeli, the likely head of the post-Peel Conservative Party, was much younger -- meaning Gladstone's own path to the premiership was likely shorter under the Liberals. The post he was offered was one of the government's top jobs, which surely eased the decision too. Hawkins notes that Gladstone made just one condition of joining Palmerston's cabinet: the Chancellorship of the Exchequer (262). This peeved many of his new colleagues, but Russell and Palmerston wanted Gladstone's tongue on their side and not the other.

Some historians think Gladstone's embrace of Italy was motivated by politics. But his ambivalence in Corfu shows he was already beginning to make an ideological switch, and the depth of his transition shows that it was not done for political expedience.

## The results of Corfu

Once the dam was broken, Gladstone began taking more liberal positions. The year after he returned from Corfu, he supported a free press by eliminating the "paper duties" that made political information available to the masses. Robert Kelley, in *The Transatlantic Persuasion*, treats this as Gladstone's real breakthrough: a tumultuous 1860 fight in which the Lords were humiliated over the repeal of the paper duties, opening up cheap newspapers -- and therefore political information -- to the masses, and winning Gladstone the lasting loyalty of the new popular press that was itself remaking British politics as the voice of rising Liberalism (Kelley 204).

Not that tracking the change in Gladstone's thought is always easy. He was cagey about political philosophy. David Bebbington, in his excellent *The Mind of Gladstone*, searches the record for a clear statement of what "Gladstonian Liberalism" actually was, and finds surprisingly little -- including a line from Gladstone himself dismissing political philosophy as not really England's strong suit. Bebbington argues Gladstone found the ancients more comfortable than the moderns with treating government as a genuine art and science, and that Christianity, in Gladstone's own view, had displaced that with a narrower preoccupation with individual ethics.

Perhaps Gladstone's view of the ancients' comfort with political philosophy is one reason that the clearest articulation of his ideological change on the key issue of the day came in his writings on Homer, not politics. It is true that Gladstone once slipped and stated support for eventual democracy on the floor of Parliament, arguing that any man not disqualified by some genuine personal unfitness or political danger was morally entitled to the vote -- before immediately backpedaling, reaching for rather thin justifications to walk the statement back.

Williams argues this was no mere slip of the tongue but the surfacing, however reluctant, of a long process already underway. And Gladstone would eventually come to describe himself in starker terms still, as Morley records in a well-known anecdote: sparring genially with Ruskin, who accused his host of being a "leveller" who believed one man's political judgment as good as another's, Gladstone shot back that he believed in nothing of the sort -- that he was, in fact, "an out-and-out inequalitarian," a confession Ruskin reportedly received with delighted, hand-clapping triumph.

A clearer view of Gladstone's transition on the subject of democracy comes in his Homeric scholarship. A few months before he went to Corfu, in his *Studies of Homer and the Homeric Age*, he wrote disparagingly of rule by simple "numbers" -- arguing that Agamemnon's forces in the Trojan War hadn't decided things by majority vote, and that Britain didn't either, and shouldn't. Decision by majority, he wrote there, was a practical expedient rather than a realization of anything like perfection -- something adopted for its usefulness, not admired for its own sake, since true authority rests only in wisdom and virtue (*Studies* 116-117).

However, when Gladstone revised that work for a later book, *Juventus Mundi*, he reversed his verdict on decisions by numbers, now attributing the "doctrine of majorities" to a more advanced stage of social development (*Juventus* 434). Historians Bebbington and Frank M. Turner have both flagged this reversal as significant (Bebbington 179, Turner 242). This change of mind on the key issue of the day is a major transition. It came just after Britain's Second Reform Act passed in 1867. While Disraeli was responsible for the passage of the final version of the act, Gladstone had fought for reform as well. And once the reforms were in effect, it was Gladstone who benefited, replacing Disraeli as prime minister on the strength of new working-class voters.

In 1864 Gladstone voted for the Ionian Islands to rejoin Greece after all. This demonstrates that the ultimate consequence of his trip to Corfu was that it increased his sympathy for self-governing peoples. His vote was on the winning side, and the islands rejoined Greece and remain part of that nation today.

An interpretation of Gladstone's move to the left as stemming from Corfu has rarely been examined in depth. Morley himself floated, only in passing, the idea that close contact with liberal ideas about self-government abroad must have accelerated Gladstone's own liberal thinking at home, but never developed the point (Morley 620). Most scholars instead emphasize the gradualness of Gladstone's transformation. Morley himself finds it "protracted." Jenkins calls his leftward drift in 1859 "crabwise" -- a sideways walk. Blake argues the real distance between politicians like Gladstone and Disraeli looks smaller to us today than it did then. Woodrow Wilson is the one notable exception, arguing Gladstone's shift was actually quick, and that his liberalism matured and strengthened rapidly -- though Wilson located the real turning point much earlier, back when Peel was first pulling the Tories toward the center, which rather undercuts the "quick" framing.

That gradualness was not imagined by scholars, but it obscures the question of where the new path originated. What hasn't been examined sufficiently is the answer to the question of whether Gladstone arrived in Corfu a conservative and left a liberal. The answer is clear: Gladstone was a Conservative at heart when he landed in the Ionian Islands, and began his path to what the world would come to know as Gladstonian Liberalism there. Ironically, it was Disraeli who suffered for it. Like the hero of his first novel, *Vivian Grey*, Dizzy had concocted a Machiavellian gambit that backfired. Gladstone became his toughest rival for decades thereafter.
